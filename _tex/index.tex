  \begin{abstract}
    Dynamic food web models provide a mechanistic framework for
    understanding ecological stability, extinction cascades, and
    responses to environmental change, but their predictions depend on
    the network supplied as model input. Empirical metawebs provide rich
    information on potential species interactions yet contain
    interactions that may not occur simultaneously within a community.
    Converting these interaction pools into realised networks therefore
    requires assumptions about which interactions are likely to
    co-occur, potentially introducing uncertainty before population
    dynamics are simulated. Here, we investigate how alternative
    approaches to network realisation influence network structure and
    subsequent ecological dynamics. Using empirical metawebs, we
    generated realised food webs using four ecologically motivated
    downsampling approaches, alongside networks generated using Niche
    and allometric trophic network models as reference approaches. We
    then compared network topology, structural change during dynamical
    burn-in, and inferred robustness under topological and dynamic
    extinction simulations. Downsampling approach altered network
    topology, with differences becoming increasingly pronounced as
    greater proportions of interactions were removed. Dynamical burn-in
    further reorganised network structure but did not erase the
    signatures introduced during network construction; instead,
    downsampled networks underwent structural changes that were often
    more like the niche model than the original metaweb. Despite these
    differences, the broad tendency for topological extinction
    simulations to infer greater robustness than dynamic simulations
    were consistent across network reconstruction approaches. However,
    dynamic simulations revealed differences in network behaviour that
    were not apparent from topology alone, including convergence of
    niche-based downsampling towards the behaviour of the synthetic
    niche model. These results demonstrate that network realisation is
    not simply a preprocessing step, but an additional source of
    ecological model uncertainty. Dynamic food web predictions are
    therefore shaped by successive assumptions about which interactions
    are realised and which of those realised networks can persist
    dynamically, highlighting the importance of explicitly considering
    network reconstruction when using empirical interaction data to make
    mechanistic predictions about ecological change.
          \par\bigskip
      \noindent\textbf{Keywords:} food webs, ecological
networks, network reconstruction, network
realisation, metawebs, dynamical modelling, extinction
cascades, ecological model uncertainty
      \end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Ecological networks provide a powerful framework for understanding how
biodiversity is organised and how ecological communities respond to
environmental change. Early food web research focused largely on
describing structural properties such as connectance, trophic
organisation, and degree distributions, revealing recurring patterns
across ecosystems (Williams \& Martinez, 2000). Increasingly, however,
attention has shifted from describing network architecture to predicting
ecological processes. Mechanistic dynamic food web models enable
researchers to investigate community stability, biomass dynamics,
extinction cascades, and responses to perturbation by explicitly
simulating energy transfer among interacting species (Brose et al.,
2006; Curtsdotter et al., 2011; Delmas et al., 2017; Yodzis \& Innes,
1992). By incorporating resource limitation, interaction strengths, and
feedback, these models provide a means of linking network structure to
ecological dynamics. Their predictions, however, depend not only on the
equations governing population dynamics, but also on the network
supplied as model input.

Constructing appropriate networks for dynamic modelling presents a
fundamental challenge. Synthetic food web models, such as the niche
model, provide tractable representations of trophic structure and can be
readily coupled to population dynamics (Williams \& Martinez, 2000).
Other approaches incorporate biological information, including
consumer--resource body-size relationships, to generate mechanistically
informed networks, as in allometric models (Brose et al., 2006; Petchey
et al., 2008). However, these approaches make assumptions about how
trophic interactions are organised and do not necessarily recover the
specific interactions occurring within a given community, limiting their
applicability to species-specific, real-world tangibility. Empirical
metawebs offer an alternative by compiling potential trophic
interactions among species from observations, literature, or trait-based
inference (Dunne, 2006). Because these interactions may have been
observed or inferred across different locations, seasons, or
environmental conditions, however, a metaweb does not necessarily
represent a set of interactions that can occur simultaneously within a
single community. Using empirical interaction pools in dynamic models
therefore requires an additional step: selecting which potential
interactions are realised together to construct a plausible
community-level food web.

This process of network realisation introduces assumptions about how
ecological processes filter potential interactions into realised
communities. Different approaches may favour interactions near the
centre of a consumer's trophic niche, assign retention probabilities
according to consumer trophic breadth, favour links involving specific
combinations of highly connected species, or remove interactions without
reference to their ecological characteristics. Although these approaches
can operate on the same empirical metaweb, they may produce networks
with different structural properties. Such differences may become
particularly important when substantial reductions in connectance are
required, because the choice of which interactions to retain
increasingly determines the architecture of the resulting network.
Network reconstruction is therefore not simply a technical step for
reducing network size or connectance, but an ecological modelling
decision that can influence subsequent inference. More broadly, concerns
about the comparability and reuse of interaction networks highlight the
potential for methodological choices to propagate into ecological
conclusions (Brimacombe et al., 2023). Yet how reconstruction
assumptions influence both the subsequent structural reorganisation and
the dynamical behaviour of food webs remains insufficiently understood.

Network construction is followed by a second filtering process when the
resulting food web is subjected to population dynamics. Dynamic models
typically undergo a burn-in period during which biomass changes and
species unable to persist under the specified model conditions are lost.
This process transforms the initial network into a dynamically viable
community, potentially altering its structure before any perturbation is
imposed. Network realisation thus occurs in two stages: the initial
selection of potential interactions and the subsequent filtering of the
constructed network through dynamical persistence. These stages may have
different consequences for ecological inference. Initial reconstruction
may determine much of the network architecture inherited by subsequent
simulations, whereas dynamical filtering may introduce additional
differences through biomass-dependent and rate-dependent processes. It
is therefore important to establish whether structural differences
introduced during network construction persist through burn-in, and
whether networks that differ in their initial topology also differ in
their responses to extinction.

This distinction is particularly relevant when comparing topological and
dynamic approaches to extinction robustness. Topological extinction
simulations infer secondary extinctions from network structure, whereas
dynamic simulations additionally account for changes in biomass and the
ecological processes governing species persistence. Previous work has
shown that these approaches can yield different estimates of food web
robustness (Curtsdotter et al., 2011), but the extent to which this
difference generalises across alternative network reconstruction
approaches remains unclear. Moreover, similarities in topological
extinction responses need not imply similarities in dynamic behaviour,
because networks with comparable structural properties may interact
differently with their functional parameterisation. Comparing network
types across both approaches can therefore distinguish conclusions that
are relatively insensitive to reconstruction choices from those that
depend on how networks are constructed and dynamically parameterised.

Understanding these dependencies is particularly important for
ecological systems in which empirical interaction data are incomplete,
including palaeoecological communities. Fossil records provide
opportunities to investigate ecological change across major extinction
events, but reconstructed assemblages rarely provide a complete
observation of a single realised food web (Dunhill et al., 2024; Fricke
et al., 2022; Karapunar et al., 2026; Roopnarine, 2017; Roopnarine,
2006). Instead, they may be more appropriately represented as
empirically constrained pools of potential interactions from which
plausible community networks must be inferred. Translating these
interaction pools into dynamically viable food webs could provide a
route towards testing mechanistic explanations of ecological change,
including through comparisons across extinction events and hindcasting
exercises (Strydom, Catchen, et al., 2021). Such applications, however,
require an understanding of how assumptions made during network
reconstruction influence the ecological behaviour subsequently inferred
from dynamic models.

Here, we investigate how alternative approaches to network realisation
influence food web structure, burn-in, and inferred extinction
robustness. Using empirical metawebs, we generate networks with four
ecologically motivated downsampling approaches and compare them with
networks constructed using the niche and allometric trophic network
models. Specifically, we ask three questions. First, how does
downsampling approach influence network topology, and to what extent do
these differences persist following burn-in? Second, does the difference
between topological and dynamic estimates of extinction robustness
generalise across network reconstruction approaches and extinction
scenarios? Third, do alternative network types retain similar
relationships in inferred robustness under topological and dynamic
extinction simulations, or does incorporating population dynamics reveal
differences that are not apparent from topology alone? Rather than
identifying a single optimal reconstruction method, we use these
comparisons to investigate how assumptions introduced during network
construction propagate through subsequent dynamical modelling. We show
that reconstruction approaches produce increasingly distinct topologies
as greater proportions of interactions are removed, that burn-in acts as
an additional structural filter, and that the relationship between
topological and dynamic robustness is broadly consistent across network
types despite differences in their inferred behaviour. Together, these
results demonstrate that network realisation is an important source of
ecological model uncertainty, while also identifying conclusions that
remain comparatively robust across alternative representations of food
web structure.

\section{Methods}\label{methods}

\subsection{Fossil data}\label{fossil-data}

We used community data from seven locations spanning the
Permian-Triassic mass extinction (PTME) event compiled by Karapunar et
al. (2026). This dataset comprises community-level trophic data
representing potentially interacting taxa across seven locations across
the Permian-Triassic mass extinction (PTME; \textasciitilde250 Ma)
event. Here we used the guild-level assemblages from the pre-extinction
(Permian) time point along with the plankton and zooplankton data from
the outputs of the EcoGEnIE models (Ward et al., 2018) also used in
Karapunar et al. (2026). These represent distinct communities of
potentially interacting guilds. We excluded the community from Russia
because it contained no intermediate species and was therefore
unsuitable for the subsequent extinction analyses as it would produce
distinct results. The seven locations act as replicates to better
determine if observed extinction patterns are driven by specific network
structures (location) or if results are more generalisable. Detailed
descriptions of the underlying community data, including trait
assignment, their composition and spatial structure, are provided in
Karapunar et al. (2026).

\subsection{Network construction}\label{network-construction}

Metawebs were constructed using the Paleo Food Web Inference Model
(PFWIM; Shaw et al. (2024)). The PFWIM applies a set of mechanistic
feasibility rules to consumer--resource pairs based on different trait
classes. An interaction is deemed as being feasible only when all trait
combinations satisfy the specified rules. These metawebs were
subsequently downsampled to the specified target connectance (see
Section~\ref{sec-downsample} for details on downsampling). Using the
community data we also constructed networks using the allometric trophic
network (ATN) model (Brose et al., 2006), which represents a realised
but empirically grounded network. For the ATN species body masses were
sampled independently from a truncated log-normal distribution
constrained by species categorical body-size classifications.
Interaction probabilities were generated by varying the feeding
threshold between 0.01 and 0.12 and selecting the network whose
connectance most closely matched a target connectance. Finally for each
community we also constructed a niche model network (Williams \&
Martinez, 2000) using the guild richness and target connectance as input
parameters (see Section~\ref{sec-simulations} for details on
parametrisation)

\subsection{Downsampling approaches}\label{sec-downsample}

We treat downsampling as an assumption on the process of ecological
realisation rather than observation. Starting from a metaweb containing
all potential trophic interactions, each downsampling approach
represents an alternative ecological hypothesis describing how local
ecological processes may filter these potential interactions into the
subset that is realised within a particular community. The different
approaches therefore differ not only in their implementation, but also
in the ecological assumptions they embed about how realised food webs
emerge from a common regional interaction pool.

\subsubsection{Niche}\label{niche}

The original niche model from Williams \& Martinez (2000) assumes that
realised interactions represent the core of a consumer's trophic niche,
and that these diets can be arranged along a single niche axis. In order
to create a downsampling approach that mimics this we infer a latent
trophic axis directly from the metaweb using Singular Value
Decomposition (SVD). It has been shown that the latent spaces described
by the left and right singular vectors are closely aligned with the
generality and vulnerability of species (Strydom et al., 2022) and so
the SVD composition should retain the dietary signal of the species.
This is done as follows:

Let \(A\) denote the \(S \times S\) adjacency matrix of the metaweb,
where \(A_{ij}=1\) if consumer \(i\) feeds on resource \(j\). We compute
its singular value decomposition,

\[
A = U \Sigma V^\top
\]

where \(U\) and \(V\) are the left and right singular vectors,
respectively, and \(\Sigma\) is the diagonal matrix of singular values.
Using the leading singular value, \(\sigma_1\), and corresponding
singular vectors, \(\mathbf{u}_1\) and \(\mathbf{v}_1\), each species is
assigned latent consumer and resource coordinates,

\[
c_i = \sqrt{\sigma_1}\,u_{i1}, \qquad r_i = \sqrt{\sigma_1}\,v_{i1}
\]

Because species can act both as consumers and as resources, these
coordinates are combined to obtain a single niche position,

\[ 
x_i = \frac{c_i + r_i}{2}
\]

which is subsequently rescaled to lie between 0 and 1. Species with
similar values of \(x_i\) therefore occupy similar positions along the
dominant trophic gradient of the metaweb.

For each consumer \(i\), the centroid of its realised niche is

\[
\mu_i = \frac{1}{k_i}\sum_{j \in R_i} x_j
\]

where \(R_i\) is the set of resources consumed by species \(i\), and
\(k_i\) is its trophic breadth (generality). Niche breadth is estimated
as the standard deviation of prey positions,

\[
\sigma_i = \max\left(sd(x_j),\,0.1\right)
\]

where the lower bound prevents unrealistically narrow niches for
specialists. Niche breadth can optionally be scaled to further constrain
realised diets,

\[
\sigma_i' = \alpha \sigma_i
\]

where \(\alpha\) is acts as a scaling parameter.

The probability of retaining an interaction between consumer \(i\) and
resource \(j\) is then

\[
P_{ij} \propto \exp\left[-\left(\frac{x_j-\mu_i}{\sigma_i'}\right)^2\right]
\]

Interactions closest to the consumer's niche centre are therefore most
likely to be realised (retained), whereas interactions towards the
margins of the niche are preferentially removed. Ecologically, this
assumes that local communities realise the central portion of a
consumer's potential trophic niche, with peripheral or opportunistic
interactions occurring less consistently.

\subsubsection{Power-law}\label{power-law}

The power-law approach assumes that interaction realisation depends
primarily on consumer trophic breadth. Following Roopnarine (2006), the
retention probability for consumer \(i\) is

\[
P_i \propto \exp\left(\frac{k_i}{E}\right)
\]

where \(k_i\) is consumer generality and

\[
E = S^{(y-1)/y}
\]

with \(S\) denoting network size and \(y\) the scaling exponent.
Probabilities are subsequently normalised to lie between 0 and 1. Every
interaction belonging to consumer \(i\) inherits the same retention
probability,

\[
P_{ij}=P_i
\]

Ecologically, this represents the hypothesis that generalist consumers
realise a larger fraction of their potential trophic niche than
specialist consumers, irrespective of the identity of individual
resources.

\subsubsection{Degree-product}\label{degree-product}

The degree-product approach assumes that interaction realisation depends
on the centrality of both interacting species. Here each interaction is
assigned a weight

\[
w_{ij}=k_i^{out}k_j^{in}
\]

where \(k_i^{out}\) is the consumer's out-degree (generality) and
\(k_j^{in}\) is the resource's in-degree (vulnerability). Retention
probabilities are then calculated as

\[
P_{ij} = 1- \frac{w_{ij}}{\max(w)}
\]

before being constrained to the interval \([0,1]\). Under iterative
downsampling, degrees are recalculated following each interaction
removal, allowing probabilities to evolve as the realised network
changes. Ecologically, this assumes that highly connected species
possess many potential interactions that are not simultaneously realised
within any local community, whereas interactions involving more
specialised species are more likely to represent essential realised
links.

\subsubsection{Random}\label{random}

Random downsampling provides the ecological null model. Every
interaction has an equal probability of being retained,

\[
P_{ij}=c
\]

where \(c\) is constant for all existing interactions. Interactions are
removed uniformly at random until the target connectance is reached,
subject to the constraint that species cannot become isolated. This
assumes that no ecological process preferentially favours the
realisation of one potential interaction over another, such that
realised food webs constitute an unbiased subset of the regional metaweb
that is ultimately only constrained by the number of links.

\subsection{Simulations}\label{sec-simulations}

For each empirical community, we generated 30 network replicates (\(n\)
= 30 replicates \(\times\) 7 network types \(\times\) 6 locations). For
each replicate a target connectance was independently sampled from a
truncated normal distribution bounded between 0.05 and 0.15. This target
connectance was used to determine the `degree of downsampling' of the
metaweb for each downsampling approach as well as constraining the ATN
and Niche networks. These represent the `creation' networks

For the dynamic simulations body masses were assigned using the
predator-prey body mass distribution where body mass (\(M_i\)) is
assigned deterministically from the trophic level (\(TL\)) as follows
\(M_{i} = Z^{TL_{i}-1}\), where \(Z\) represents the assumed constant
predator--prey body-mass ratio, here set to 10. Note that even for the
ATN networks we used this theoretical body size specification despite
the network itself being constructed with empirical bodysizes. This is
because we found that for networks which had empirically specified body
sizes the community would almost completely collapse (see Supplementary
Materials), which suggests the dynamic model itself is sensitive to this
specification. Initial biomasses were drawn from a uniform distribution.
These trait values were used to construct a series of alternative
networks.

Each creation network was then parameterised using the default
parameters (Delmas et al., 2017) and ran the model until we had stable
biomass fluctuation or up to 5000 time steps. Species whose biomass
declined below the survival threshold (\(10^{-12}\)) during this burn-in
period were considered extinct and removed from the network. The
surviving species and their interactions constituted the corresponding
`realised' networks.

For each `creation' and `realised' network we measured 32 network
structural metrics (described in Table S1). These span a different
components of network structure from macro-scale to node-level
properties.

\subsubsection{Extinction simulations}\label{extinction-simulations}

For extinctions we followed the framework of Curtsdotter et al. (2011)
and simulated both topological and dynamic extinctions. Topological
extinctions were done on both the `creation' as well as `realised'
networks. For each type of extinction simulation we used the same ten
extinction scenarios from Curtsdotter et al. (2011) to determine the
order of species to be removed for primary extinctions. For topological
extinctions secondary extinctions occurred when species lost all prey,
these secondary extinctions were allowed to cascade before the next
primary extinction occurred. For dynamic extinctions primary extinctions
were imposed by setting the biomass of the focal species to the survival
threshold (\(10^{-12}\)), after which the community was simulated for a
further 5000 time steps to allow for the downstream effects of the
primary extinction to propagate through the system. Species whose
biomass subsequently declined below the survival threshold were recorded
as secondary extinctions and removed at the end of the 5000 time steps.
This procedure was repeated until the entire community collapsed.

For each extinction scenario, robustness was quantified from the
resulting extinction curves as the proportion of primary extinctions
required to produce a 50\% reduction in total species richness
(\(R_{50}\) (Jonsson et al., 2015)). In instances when a primary
extinction would `overshoot' the 50\% robustness was estimated by linear
interpolation between extinction events.

\section{Downsampling and burn-in alter network topology in
method-dependent
ways}\label{downsampling-and-burn-in-alter-network-topology-in-method-dependent-ways}

Redundancy analysis (RDA), using permutation-based significance testing
while accounting for community-level variation, revealed that
downsampling approach significantly affected network topology, but that
this effect depended on the magnitude of connectance reduction required
to reach the target connectance (999 permutations; \(F_{7, 707}\) =
112.8, \(p\) = 0.001; Figure~\ref{fig-downsample}, panel A). The type of
network (\emph{i.e.,} downsampling approach) explained 31.8\% of total
topology variation (adjusted \(R^2\) = 0.318), with community identity
accounting for an additional 39.7\% of variation. The significant
interaction between downsampling approach and connectance change
(marginal test; \(F_{3, 707}\) = 18.90, \(p\) = 0.001) demonstrated that
topology trajectories differed among downsampling methods depending on
the degree of downsampling required. Thus, the extent to which network
structure diverged from the original topology depended on both the
severity of downsampling and the mechanism by which interactions were
removed.

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio]{figures/downsamplingEffect.png}}

}

\caption{\label{fig-downsample}\textbf{Downsampling approach influences
both network topology and subsequent structural reorganisation during
burn-in.} (A) Redundancy analysis (RDA) ordination showing differences
in network topology among downsampling approaches across the downsamping
(\(\Delta Co\)) gradient after accounting for community-level variation.
(B) Principal component trajectories illustrating structural change
between network creation and burn-in for each downsampling approach. (C)
Loadings of the top ten contributing structure metrics.}

\end{figure}%

We next asked whether these structurally distinct networks responded
differently during the burn-in phase (Figure~\ref{fig-downsample}, panel
B). Structural reorganisation was quantified as the Euclidean
displacement between network positions before and after burn-in in the
first three principal component dimensions, with location included as a
random effect to account for the substantial variation among location.
Burn-in displacement differed significantly among network types (\(F\) =
62.0, \(p\) \textless{} 0.001). Metawebs underwent the greatest
structural reorganisation, whereas ATN networks exhibited the smallest
displacement. Among the downsampled networks, all but the randomly
downsampled networks had a displacement similar to that of the Niche
model (Figure S2), while all downsampled networks showed displacement
distinct from the original metaweb. When looking at the loadings of the
first two PC axes (Figure~\ref{fig-downsample}, panel C), the primary
changes in structure were related to node-level measures and,
unsurprisingly, the number of links. This is to be expected since there
is no rewiring of links permitted in this system, and as such any
changes should be driven by the loss of nodes rather than a major
reorganisation of network structure.

Together, these analyses demonstrate that downsampling approach
influences network topology both during the initial downsampling step
and during the subsequent burn-in phase. Differences among downsampling
methods become increasingly pronounced as greater proportions of
interactions are removed, consistent with the expectation that the
ecological assumptions underlying each removal strategy would become
more embedded as more links are removed. Importantly, despite generating
distinct initial topologies, the different downsampling approaches
underwent remarkably similar structural reorganisation during burn-in,
changing in ways more similar to the niche model than the original
metaweb. This suggests that ecologically informed downsampling of
metawebs allows us to produce networks whose subsequent structure more
closely resembles that of realised networks, while also suggesting that
the burn-in phase is, in and of itself, an additional network
realisation step. Notably, the two realised models (Niche and ATN) do
not behave similarly and still end up at different `end points'
following burn-in compared with the empirical webs, highlighting that
the choice of network reconstruction approach will still have a strong
effect on the structure and behaviour of the system (Strydom et al.,
2026).

\section{Differences in inferred robustness of topological and dynamic
extinctions are unaffected by network
type}\label{differences-in-inferred-robustness-of-topological-and-dynamic-extinctions-are-unaffected-by-network-type}

To determine whether the qualitative differences between topological and
dynamic extinction simulations previously demonstrated by Curtsdotter et
al. (2011) generalise across alternative network reconstructions, we
compared the difference in robustness (\(\Delta R_{50}\)) among network
types for each extinction scenario while accounting for community-level
variation. Network type significantly affected \(\Delta R_{50}\) across
extinction scenarios (model summaries in Table S2), indicating that the
magnitude of the difference between topological and dynamic robustness
depended on network reconstruction.

Despite these quantitative differences, the qualitative pattern
identified by Curtsdotter et al. (2011) was largely maintained. Under
all but random basal removal, topological extinction inferred greater
robustness than dynamic extinction for every network type, although the
magnitude of this discrepancy varied among reconstruction methods
(Figure~\ref{fig-TopoVDynamic}). Random basal extinction represented the
only notable departure from this general pattern (Figure S3). Here, the
discrepancy between topological and dynamic robustness was substantially
reduced, with power-law and degree-product downsampled networks
exhibiting a slightly higher \(\Delta R_{50}\) and random downsampled
networks showing little difference between the two approaches. Thus, the
conclusion that topological analyses provide more conservative estimates
of robustness, although not universal, remains remarkably robust across
reconstruction methods and extinction scenarios.

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio]{figures/robustnessTopoVDynamic.png}}

}

\caption{\label{fig-TopoVDynamic}\textbf{Differences between topological
and dynamic estimates of network robustness.} Boxplot showing aggregated
realised topological and dynamic robustness across the different
communities and extinction scenarios for the different network types.}

\end{figure}%

Together, these analyses demonstrate that the principal conclusion of
Curtsdotter et al. (2011) extends well beyond the niche model. While the
precise magnitude of the discrepancy between topological and dynamic
robustness depends on both network reconstruction and extinction
mechanism, the qualitative tendency for topological extinction
simulations to infer greater robustness than dynamic simulations were
recovered across most reconstructed food webs examined here.

\section{Dynamic extinctions re-enforce ecological
assumptions}\label{dynamic-extinctions-re-enforce-ecological-assumptions}

For each community \(\times\) extinction scenario \(\times\) time point,
\(R_{50}\) was modelled as a function of network type, and estimated
marginal means were compared using Tukey-adjusted pairwise contrasts
(Figure S4-6). Network-type pairs with adjusted \(p \geq\) 0.05 were
considered indistinguishable, and the frequency of indistinguishable
comparisons was summarised across scenarios (Figure S7). To identify
broader groupings of network types, estimated marginal means were
standardised, Euclidean distances among network types were calculated,
and hierarchical clustering was performed using Ward's \(D^2\) method
(Figure~\ref{fig-robDynamic}).

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio]{figures/robustnessClusters.png}}

}

\caption{\label{fig-robDynamic}\textbf{Clustering of network types based
on similarity in inferred robustness (\(R_{50}\)).} Dendrograms show
hierarchical clustering of network types based on their estimated
marginal mean (\(R_{50}\)) values across community (\times) extinction
scenario combinations. Clustering was performed separately for each
extinction time point after standardising estimated marginal means

\end{figure}%

In terms of topological robustness, metaweb derived networks
consistently formed a distinct cluster separate from the two realised
networks (niche model and ATN; Figure~\ref{fig-robDynamic}, top two
rows). However, under dynamic extinction simulations, this grouping
broke down, with a reshuffling of the relative similarity among network
types (Figure~\ref{fig-robDynamic}, bottom row). Most notably, the niche
downsampled networks converged with those of the Niche model, whereas
the remaining empirically derived networks maintained their mutual
similarity.

Topological extinction simulations primarily interrogate network
architecture and therefore retain the common structural signature
inherited from the metaweb. Dynamic simulations introduce biomass- and
rate-dependent interactions, revealing differences among networks that
are not apparent from topology alone. When examining topological
extinctions, it is clear why empirically derived networks behave
similarly to one another: they share a common baseline architecture
inherited from the regional metaweb (Figure~\ref{fig-downsample}, panel
B), and topological cascades depend strictly on network topology. For
dynamic extinctions, however, network behaviour is not determined solely
by initial structure. Instead, higher-order differences emerge from how
model parameterisation interacts with network topology. Thus, network
structure appears sufficient to explain much of the similarity in
responses to topological extinctions, but is insufficient to predict
responses to dynamic extinctions, where functional parameterisation
introduces additional sources of variation. Although the clustering
pattern was not universal across communities (Figures S8-10), suggesting
that initial network structure still exerts an influence.

This raises an important question about the role of network connectance
-- to what extent does specifying connectance constrain the structural
and dynamical properties of generated networks? Work from Poisot \&
Gravel (2014) and Strydom, Dalla Riva, et al. (2021) shows that
less-connected networks can exhibit greater structural complexity,
because there are many ways to arrange a small number of links but
progressively fewer ways to arrange links as a network becomes fully
connected. At low connectance, we therefore expect a much larger number
of possible network configurations, meaning that in specifying
connectance we place constraints on the precise structure of the
resulting network. This provides one possible explanation for why
increasingly aggressive downsampling can amplify differences among
reconstruction approaches: as fewer interactions are retained, the
ecological assumptions used to determine which interactions remain have
greater scope to shape the resulting network.

\section{Conclusions}\label{conclusions}

Reconstructing realised food webs from regional interaction pools
requires assumptions about which potential interactions are likely to
occur together. Here, we demonstrate that alternative downsampling
approaches can produce dynamically viable food webs from the same
empirical metaweb, but that the resulting networks retain detectable
signatures of the assumptions underlying their construction. These
differences become increasingly pronounced as greater proportions of
interactions are removed, indicating that downsampling is not a neutral
step between empirical data collection and dynamical modelling.

Our results further suggest that dynamical burn-in represents a second
form of network realisation. Although burn-in altered the structure of
all networks, it did not erase the differences introduced during initial
network construction. Instead, the dynamical model acted as an
additional filter, determining which structurally feasible networks
could persist under its assumptions about biomass, interaction
strengths, and species persistence. The ecological behaviour of a
simulated community is therefore shaped by successive filters. First,
how potential interactions are realised within a network, and second,
how that network is filtered into a dynamically viable community.

Despite these differences in network structure and dynamical behaviour,
the broad relationship between topological and dynamic robustness was
consistent across network reconstruction approaches. Topological
extinction simulations generally indicated greater robustness than
dynamic simulations, consistent with previous work (Curtsdotter et al.,
2011). This suggests that some conclusions drawn from network topology
may be relatively robust to uncertainty in network reconstruction
(Strydom et al., 2026). However, dynamic simulations also revealed
differences among networks that were not apparent from topology alone,
demonstrating that networks generated from the same empirical
interaction pool can exhibit different dynamical behaviour depending on
the assumptions used to realise that pool.

These findings highlight an important challenge for the growing use of
dynamic food web models to move beyond describing network structure
towards predicting ecological change. Increasingly sophisticated
approaches can reconstruct networks from empirical observations or
biological traits, while regional metawebs provide a means of retaining
broad empirical information about potential interactions. Yet neither
empirical provenance nor mechanistic parameterisation necessarily
produces a realised community as both require assumptions about which
interactions occur together and (in dynamic models) which of those
interactions can persist. Network realisation therefore represents a
necessary but currently underappreciated bridge between empirical
interaction data and mechanistic dynamic modelling.

Developing a clear framework for explicitly incorporating empirical
metawebs into dynamic models is an important step towards bridging
theoretical work on network stability with real-world communities and
ecological scenarios. Our case study reinforces the findings of
Curtsdotter et al. (2011), suggesting that topological extinction
simulations alone may underestimate the importance of top-down effects
in determining secondary extinctions and community robustness.
Intentional downsampling provides a means of approximating the structure
of realised communities (Roopnarine, 2006), helping to integrate
ecological realism with theoretical predictions. If palaeoecological
communities can be reconstructed as plausible, dynamically viable food
webs, dynamic modelling could provide a route to testing mechanistic
explanations of observed ecological change rather than relying solely on
topological reconstructions. Our results represent a first step towards
this goal, while demonstrating that the assumptions underlying network
construction can remain detectable throughout the modelling process.

Rather than identifying a single correct method for constructing
realised networks, we argue that network realisation should be
recognised as a methodological choice that can influence model outcomes.
We therefore recommend that researchers who use downsampling explicitly
test the sensitivity of their results to alternative downsampling
approaches. As food web modelling increasingly combines empirical
interaction data with dynamic approaches, predictions will depend not
only on the equations governing population dynamics, but also on how
empirical knowledge is translated into the networks on which those
equations operate. Understanding and quantifying this additional source
of uncertainty will be essential if dynamic food web models are to
provide increasingly empirically grounded predictions of how ecological
communities respond to environmental change.
